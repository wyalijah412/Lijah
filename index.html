“The Dead” · the window on the snow

A window at each end of the book

Joyce's Dubliners opens its adolescent stories with a girl at a window and ends the whole book with a man at a window. In “Eveline” a young woman sits watching the evening invade the avenue, deciding whether to leave Dublin forever. In “The Dead” Gabriel Conroy stands at a hotel window watching the snow, after learning something about his wife that undoes him.

Both stories build toward an epiphany. Eveline's ends in paralysis: she cannot move. Gabriel's ends in release: his old self dissolves. This site lets you step through each story to its moment of revelation, then read both moments together in one poem.

Key term

epiphany

e·piph·a·ny · from Greek epiphaneia, “manifestation”

  1. In the Church calendar, January 6: the feast of Christ's manifestation to the Magi.
  2. In Joyce: “a sudden spiritual manifestation,” when an ordinary word, gesture or object suddenly reveals the truth of a life. (Stephen Hero)

At the window

She sat at the window watching the evening invade the avenue.“Eveline,” first sentence

Eveline is over nineteen. She works at the Stores, hands her wages to a violent father and minds the younger children. Looking round the room at the familiar objects she has dusted for years, she wonders where all the dust comes from. Tonight she means to leave.

The party

West Briton!Miss Ivors, whispering to Gabriel

At his aunts' annual dance, Gabriel Conroy carves the goose and gives the after-dinner speech, toasting his two aunts and their niece Mary Jane as the Three Graces. He is anxious and a little superior. Miss Ivors teases him for writing for a pro-British paper and for holidaying on the Continent instead of in the west of Ireland.

Two epiphanies, side by side

The stories mirror each other closely. Both turn on a window, a voice from the dead and a call to go somewhere far away, and they end in opposite places.

Artist's statement

In Stephen Hero, the early draft of A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man, Joyce borrows the Church's word epiphany for “a sudden spiritual manifestation”: a moment when an ordinary sight or sentence suddenly shows the truth of a life. Dubliners (1914) is built on these moments. Joyce told his publisher that he set the book in Dublin because the city seemed to him “the centre of paralysis.” I chose “Eveline” and “The Dead” because they show the two directions an epiphany can go. Eveline reaches the edge of a new life and freezes. Gabriel Conroy is changed completely.

Why two windows

“Eveline” opens with her sitting at a window, and “The Dead,” the last story in the book, ends with Gabriel standing at one. I built the whole site as a diptych of those two windows. Dust drifts in Eveline's dusk light, because she keeps wondering where all the dust in the house comes from. Snow falls in Gabriel's night, because snow is the image his epiphany ends on.

The collages

Each collage is made only of things Joyce actually puts in the story: the yellowing photograph of the priest, the broken harmonium, the street organ and her mother's last words for Eveline; the goose, the speech, the painting Gabriel would call “Distant Music,” and the boy from the gasworks for Gabriel. Stepping through the four moments lights up the objects in order, so the viewer sees the pressure build. At the epiphany each collage changes. In “Eveline” the sea rises and iron bars drop over everything, because “all the seas of the world” overwhelm her and all she can do is grip the railing. In “The Dead” the snow thickens until the collage fades to grey, the way Gabriel's “own identity was fading out.”

The poem

“Two Windows” is a contrapuntal poem. The left column follows Eveline, the right column follows Gabriel, and the lines can also be read straight across as one poem. I chose this form because the stories mirror each other. Each character hears a voice from the dead (Derevaun Seraun! and I think he died for me), each is called away, and each ends holding on to something. The poem's last line is the difference between them.

What I found

An epiphany in Joyce does not always belong to the character. Eveline never says what she has realised, so the reader has to have the epiphany for her. Gabriel's is the opposite: we watch him see himself as “a ludicrous figure” and then let that self go. Putting the two side by side shows how Dubliners moves from paralysis near its beginning to a kind of release at its very end.
